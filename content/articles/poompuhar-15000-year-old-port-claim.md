Off the Tamil Nadu coast, researchers have mapped unusual shapes on the seabed near Poompuhar, the historic port also known as Puhar or Kaveripattinam. Some have interpreted these features as an enormous harbour complex established **11,000–15,000 years ago**. If confirmed, a port of that age would radically change the history of seafaring and settled life in South Asia.

It has **not been confirmed**. The offshore features are real observations, but their interpretation as human-built structures—and especially their proposed age—remains unsettled. No securely excavated building material from the deep-water site has yet been directly dated to 15,000 years ago.

## The well-established Poompuhar

Poompuhar stands at the mouth of the Kaveri on the Bay of Bengal. Tamil works including *Pattinappalai*, *Silappadikaram* and *Manimekalai* remember it as a thriving port with markets, ships and merchants. Archaeological work has found brick structures, ring wells, a wharf and other remains on land and in shallow water. These fit an important port of the Early Historic and Sangam worlds roughly two thousand years ago.

Parts of that settlement were lost as the shoreline retreated. Underwater surveys begun in the twentieth century identified structures and a shipwreck as far as about eight kilometres offshore, while studies of coastal processes documented continuing erosion and submergence. This younger, historic port and its partial loss to the sea are not the controversial part of the story.

## Where the 15,000-year figure came from

In 2017, a team from Bharathidasan University reconstructed former coastlines using a global sea-level curve and the General Bathymetric Chart of the Oceans (GEBCO). At the end of the last Ice Age, sea level was much lower than it is today. Their model placed the coast about 30 kilometres east-southeast of modern Poompuhar around 20,000–15,000 years before present, near an ancient lobe of the Kaveri delta.

The researchers proposed that an early Poompuhar might have occupied this now-drowned delta before shifting landward in stages as the sea rose. A 2020 study combined newer GEBCO data with multibeam echo-sounder measurements. At depths of roughly 70–80 metres, it mapped features the authors interpreted as breakwaters, channels, dock-like basins and other parts of a planned harbour, spread across an area about 11 kilometres long and 2–3 kilometres wide.

The proposed date came chiefly from matching the feature’s depth and location to a model of past sea levels and shorelines. In other words, the work asked when this ground would have been exposed land. That provides a **geological age estimate for the drowned landscape**, not a direct archaeological date for construction or occupation.

## Why the claim remains unsettled

Bathymetry and sonar are valuable survey tools, but they record shape and acoustic response. Submerged river channels, ridges, sediment bodies and eroded rock can produce regular-looking patterns. Before a seabed anomaly can be identified securely as architecture, marine archaeologists ordinarily seek close visual inspection, detailed mapping, excavated cultural material and dates tied directly to an undisturbed archaeological context.

For the deep-water Poompuhar features, several decisive steps remain outstanding:

- confirming through remotely operated vehicles, diving or excavation that the forms are built rather than natural;
- recovering artefacts or construction material in secure association with them;
- directly dating those materials instead of inferring their age from sea level;
- publishing enough independent evidence for other specialists to test the interpretation.

There is also a question of historical plausibility. Fifteen thousand years ago falls near the end of the Palaeolithic, long before currently accepted evidence for urban ports anywhere in the world. That does not make the claim impossible, but it means the evidence must be exceptionally strong. A harbour implies organised construction, sustained settlement, boats, labour coordination and trade; sonar geometry alone cannot establish that entire social system.

## An open question, not an established date

The responsible conclusion is therefore twofold. First, Poompuhar is unquestionably an important historic Tamil port in a coastline repeatedly reshaped by erosion, flooding and rising seas. Second, the proposed 15,000-year-old precursor is an intriguing hypothesis awaiting direct archaeological confirmation.

Further deep-sea survey, seabed coring and targeted excavation could strengthen, revise or reject the idea. Until then, it is more accurate to say that researchers have mapped **possible harbour-like features on a landscape submerged since the end of the Ice Age** than to call Poompuhar a proven 15,000-year-old port.
