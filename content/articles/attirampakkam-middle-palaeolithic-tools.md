Attirampakkam is famous for stone tools more than a million years old, but its younger deposits tell an equally important story. Around **385,000 years ago**, toolmakers at the site began moving away from the large handaxes and cleavers of the Acheulean towards smaller, deliberately prepared flakes. This early Middle Palaeolithic transition occurred far earlier than archaeologists once expected in South Asia.

The date is sometimes rounded to “about 300,000 years ago,” but the published estimate is **385 ± 64 thousand years ago**. That range matters: prehistoric dates are measurements with uncertainty, not exact anniversaries.

## A changing toolkit

In the younger layers, large cutting tools became rare. Toolmakers increasingly produced small flakes from carefully prepared stone cores and shaped some into points and scrapers. Among their methods was Levallois technology, in which a core was prepared so that a final, planned blow detached a flake of a desired size and shape.

This was not an overnight replacement of one tool “culture” by another. The excavated sequence shows a gradual reorganisation. Some Acheulean elements persisted as prepared-core and flake technologies became dominant. Archaeologists therefore describe a transition in behaviour rather than a sharp boundary.

## Dating the transition

Researchers dated the sediments with post-infrared infrared-stimulated luminescence, or pIR-IRSL. Minerals such as feldspar store energy produced by natural radiation while buried. Exposure to daylight resets the signal; after burial it begins accumulating again. Measuring the stored signal can estimate how long the sediment has been shielded from light.

The luminescence chronology places the beginning of transitional Middle Palaeolithic processes at **385 ± 64 thousand years ago**. The Middle Palaeolithic occupation continued until about **172 ± 41 thousand years ago**, and evidence at the site diminished by roughly 74,000 years ago. The study analysed more than 7,200 artefacts from the stratified sequence and was published in *Nature* in 2018.

## What it does—and does not—tell us

The result overturned a much younger conventional chronology for India’s Middle Palaeolithic. It also placed technological change at Attirampakkam broadly alongside changes occurring in parts of Africa and Europe during the Middle Pleistocene.

That similarity does not reveal who made the tools. Middle Palaeolithic technologies are often discussed in connection with *Homo sapiens*, but no hominin fossils were found in the relevant Attirampakkam layers. The site alone cannot show whether the new methods arrived with a migrating population, spread as ideas between existing populations, or developed locally from the long Acheulean tradition.

This caution is especially important for “Out of Africa” narratives. The Attirampakkam evidence demonstrates early technological change in India; it does not by itself demonstrate the presence of modern humans there 385,000 years ago. The researchers presented several possible demographic and cultural explanations rather than choosing one unsupported scenario.

What the site securely provides is a rare, long sequence in one place. The older deposits show early Acheulean bifaces; the younger ones record the gradual rise of prepared-core and flake production. Read together, they reveal that South Asia was an active part of the deep human story, not a late or technologically isolated margin.
