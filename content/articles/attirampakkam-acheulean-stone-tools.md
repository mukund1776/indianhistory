About 60 kilometres northwest of Chennai, a shallow stream crosses a landscape of red earth and weathered rock near the village of Attirampakkam. The place looks modest, but its buried sediments preserve one of South Asia’s longest records of early stone-tool making. The oldest securely dated levels transformed the chronology of the Indian Palaeolithic: hominins were making Acheulean tools here at least **1.07 million years ago**, and the best estimate for the dated deposits is about **1.51 ± 0.07 million years**.

## What archaeologists found

Attirampakkam entered archaeological history in 1863, when geologist Robert Bruce Foote discovered stone tools there. Modern excavations began in 1999 under archaeologists Shanti Pappu and Kumar Akhilesh of the Sharma Centre for Heritage Education. Their trenches exposed a sequence roughly nine metres deep, with stone artefacts sealed inside ancient layers rather than scattered only on the modern surface.

The oldest levels contained Acheulean technology: large cutting tools shaped on both faces, especially handaxes and cleavers, alongside flakes, small tools and the waste left by knapping. A handaxe was not an axe with a handle. It was a carefully shaped piece of stone with durable cutting edges, useful for a range of tasks. Its manufacture required the maker to select raw material, control a sequence of blows and maintain an intended form.

## How the tools were dated

Stone itself cannot usually be dated by radiocarbon methods, and these deposits are far beyond radiocarbon’s practical range. The research team therefore combined two independent approaches.

First, palaeomagnetic measurements recorded the direction of Earth’s magnetic field preserved in the sediments. A reversal in the sequence helped place the lower deposits in the Early Pleistocene. Second, six stone artefacts were analysed using the cosmogenic nuclides aluminium-26 and beryllium-10. Quartz accumulates these isotopes while exposed near the ground surface; once deeply buried, their changing ratio acts as a burial clock.

The resulting ages placed the earliest Acheulean horizon at **no younger than 1.07 million years**, with a pooled average burial age of **1.51 million years**. The study, published in *Science* in 2011, made Attirampakkam the earliest securely dated Acheulean site then known in South Asia.

## Why the date matters

Before this work, the South Asian Acheulean was often assigned largely to the Middle Pleistocene, less than about 800,000 years ago. Attirampakkam pushed a well-dated Acheulean presence in India hundreds of thousands of years deeper into the past. It showed that the makers of large bifacial tools reached—or developed this technology in—southern Asia surprisingly early in the Acheulean’s wider history.

The tools do not identify their makers. No hominin fossils were recovered from these oldest layers, so assigning them confidently to *Homo erectus* or another species would go beyond the evidence. What survives is behaviour: repeated visits, knowledge of local stone, and skilled production across a landscape beside the ancient Kortallaiyar river system.

Attirampakkam therefore records more than a single dramatic arrival. Its sequence suggests a long-lived technological tradition maintained by many generations. Later layers would eventually document a major shift away from classic Acheulean handaxes—a second story, hundreds of thousands of years younger, preserved at the same site.
